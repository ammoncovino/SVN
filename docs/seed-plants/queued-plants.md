## PLANT 12 — Biosphere
### We pay twice as much for health care and die sooner.

The U.S. spent $14,775 per person on health care in 2024. Similar wealthy countries averaged $7,860, and their people live almost four years longer: 82.7 years against our 79.0.

A big part of the gap is prices. Private insurers pay hospitals about two and a half times what Medicare pays for the same care.

A fix that's been tried: Maryland gives each hospital a set yearly budget, and every insurer pays the same rates. It saved Medicare $781 million over three years and cut unplanned readmissions by about 10%.

Why it helps: hospitals stop making money by doing more, and start making money by keeping people well.

Should every state try it?

## PLANT 13 — Biosphere
### Prices went up 30% since 2020, and they're not coming back down.

From early 2020 to August 2026, prices rose about 30%. Gas is up 65%, car insurance 47%, groceries 32%.

The part nobody explains: when inflation "slows," prices don't drop. They just rise more slowly. Whether you're okay depends on whether your paycheck caught up, and for a lot of people it hasn't.

A fix that's been tried: Belgium automatically raises wages when prices rise. It protects paychecks.

The catch: it can keep inflation going longer, and employers carry the cost.

Should pay rise automatically with prices?

## PLANT 14 — Biosphere
### We now spend more on interest than on Medicare.

Last year the federal government paid over $1 trillion just in interest on its debt. That's more than Medicare ($992 billion) and more than the military ($868 billion).

Debt is on track to hit 120% of the whole economy by 2036, past the World War II record.

A fix that's been tried: Switzerland's voters put a "debt brake" in their constitution in 2001. Spending can't run ahead of revenue over the ups and downs of the economy, with an exception for real emergencies. Swiss debt went down, and it now sits around 30% of their economy.

Why it helps: it forces the hard choice every year instead of handing it to our kids.

The catch: it doesn't decide whether to cut spending or raise taxes. We'd still have to choose.

Would you vote for a debt brake?

## PLANT 15 — Biosphere
### A house now costs five years of income. It used to cost three.

In the 1990s, the typical home cost about three years of the typical household's income. In 2024 it cost five.

Half of all renters now spend more than 30% of their pay on rent.

Something that worked right here in Texas: Austin let people build more homes on smaller lots and permitted a lot of apartments. Its housing grew 30% in ten years, and median rent fell from $1,546 to $1,296.

Why it helps: when there are more homes, landlords compete for you instead of you competing for them.

The catch: most of the new homes are apartments, and the lowest incomes feel it last.

Should your city let more homes get built?

## PLANT 16 — Biosphere
### Overdose deaths are falling. Most people with addiction still get no treatment.

Overdose deaths have dropped about 35% from their 2023 peak, from around 110,000 a year to around 72,000. That's real progress.

But only about 1 in 5 adults with opioid addiction gets the medication that's proven to help.

What's working: letting doctors prescribe treatment by telehealth, and putting naloxone, the overdose-reversal spray, everywhere. People survived in 97% of reported naloxone uses.

Why it helps: getting treatment shouldn't depend on living near a clinic.

Should treatment be as easy to get as the drugs?

## PLANT 17 — Biosphere
### Most gun deaths aren't murders.

In 2024, about 44,000 Americans died from guns. 62% were suicides. 35% were homicides.

That matters, because a fix for one doesn't fix the other.

The measure with the strongest evidence, according to a RAND review of 207 studies: child-access-prevention laws, which hold adults responsible when a kid gets hold of a carelessly stored gun. They reduce youth suicides and accidental deaths.

Why it helps: it saves kids' lives without taking anyone's gun.

The catch: gun-rights groups say storage rules can slow access in an emergency.

Where do you land?

## PLANT 18 — Biosphere
### Most violent crimes never end in an arrest.

Good news first: murder fell 18% in 2025, the biggest one-year drop on record.

The problem underneath: only about half of murders get solved, and about 1 in 4 robberies.

Research says the chance of getting caught stops crime more than the length of the sentence does.

A fix that's been tried: Boston added detectives, training, and reviews for homicide cases. Its solve rate went from 47% to 57%.

Why it helps: catching people is what actually deters crime.

Should we pay for more detectives before we pay for longer sentences?

## PLANT 19 — Biosphere
### Border crossings hit a 50-year low. The courts are still years behind.

Border Patrol encounters fell from 2.2 million in 2022 to about 238,000 in 2025, the lowest since 1970.

But the immigration courts still have millions of pending cases, and an asylum case can take more than four years. Meanwhile a skilled worker from India filing for a green card today faces a projected wait of up to 179 years, because of per-country caps.

A fix that's been tried: a fast-track asylum docket in 2022 got decisions down to about nine months.

The catch: faster cases meant fewer people had lawyers. It only works with enough judges.

What would you fix first: the border, the courts, or the legal line?

## PLANT 20 — Biosphere
### AI is starting to hit entry-level jobs.

Most jobs haven't changed yet. But a Stanford study of payroll data found that workers aged 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI lost about 13% of their employment since late 2022. Older workers in the same jobs didn't.

Meanwhile, workers' share of what the economy produces is the lowest ever recorded.

A fix that's been tried: wage insurance. If you lose your job and take a lower-paying one, it covers half the gap for two years. It got people back to work faster, and it paid for itself.

Should we have it for people who lose jobs to AI?

## PLANT 1 — Biosphere
### Should Supreme Court justices serve 18 years instead of for life?

Federal judges serve for life, and picking them has turned into a political war.

The Wall Street Journal found 137 federal judges who ruled in cases where they owned stock in one of the companies involved.

A fix with wide support: 18-year terms for Supreme Court justices, with one new appointment every two years. Polls show 67% to 78% support, across both parties.

Why it helps: every president gets the same number of picks, so each seat stops being a life-or-death fight.

Would you support it?

## PLANT 2 — Biosphere
### Why we're stuck with two parties.

It's not because Americans only have two opinions. It's the voting system: one winner per district, and whoever gets the most votes wins. That math pushes everything into two parties.

In 2024, only 37 of 435 House races were close, within 5 points. Most members only fear their own party's primary.

A fix that's been tried: Alaska's open primaries with ranked-choice voting, where you rank the candidates. Alaskans voted to keep it in 2024, 51 to 49.

Why it helps: you can vote for who you actually want without "wasting" your vote.

Would you want to rank your candidates?

## PLANT 3 — Biosphere
### Churches are the only nonprofits that don't have to show their books.

Every other nonprofit files a public tax return, Form 990, showing what it spends. Churches don't have to, and their political spending isn't reported as lobbying.

Reported religious lobbying was $5.14 million in 2024. One Catholic bishops' organization alone spends about $26.7 million a year on policy work.

A fix: require religious organizations to file the same public form every other nonprofit files.

Why it helps: this isn't about faith. It's about everyone playing by the same transparency rules.

Fair, or overreach?

## PLANT 4 — Biosphere
### Your zip code decides how good your kid's school is.

Schools get much of their money from local property taxes. Rich neighborhoods get rich schools.

Spending per student ranges from about $10,000 in Idaho to over $30,000 in New York. And in underfunded districts, $1,000 more per student measurably raises graduation rates.

A fix: fund every student in a state equally, instead of by local property values.

Why it helps: a kid's chances shouldn't depend on the price of the houses around them.

Should school money follow the child instead of the zip code?

## PLANT 5 — Forest (Texas)
### Austin pays less for power than most of Texas. Here's why.

Austin's electric company is owned by the city. Customers of city-owned utilities in Texas pay 20% to 30% less than people in the deregulated market.

The catch: the city takes $115 million a year from the electric company for its general budget, and the city council sets its own rates.

And after Winter Storm Uri, about $16 billion in extra charges were billed after the shortage was already over. Ratepayers are still paying for it.

A fix: put an independent board between the city council and the rates.

Who should set your electric rates?

## PLANT 6 — Forest (Texas)
### Why does it take 45 years to rebuild 8 miles of I-35?

Planning for the I-35 rebuild through downtown Austin started in the late 1980s. It's due to finish around 2032. Cost: $4.5 billion, or $562 million a mile.

Environmental review alone takes six to seven years on a typical highway project.

A fix that's been proposed: cap environmental review at two years, and waive some federal labor-cost rules on smaller projects.

Why it helps: we'd build roads in years instead of decades.

The catch: rushing the review could miss real environmental harm.

Would you trade a faster review for some risk?

## PLANT 7 — Biosphere
### Lawsuits cost every American, even if you never sue anyone.

The U.S. lawsuit system costs about $529 billion a year, 2.6 times the rate in Europe. 43% of that goes to lawyers and overhead, not to the people who got hurt.

When Texas reformed its lawsuit rules in 2003, malpractice insurance fell 30% to 55% and more doctors moved here. But health care costs didn't drop.

A fix: "loser pays," like England, where the losing side covers the winner's legal costs. When Florida tried it, weak cases dropped and real ones won more.

Should the loser pay?

## PLANT 8 — Biosphere
### The government stamps 49 million things "classified" every year.

Experts estimate 50% to 90% of it shouldn't be secret. Government auditors found a 34% error rate.

Public records requests are supposed to be answered in 20 business days. The rate of requests fully granted has fallen to about 12%.

A fix: review everything for release after 10 years instead of 25, and release it automatically unless there's a real reason not to.

Why it helps: we can't hold anyone accountable for what we're not allowed to see.

Should government secrets expire?

## PLANT 9 — Biosphere
### Congress runs on talk instead of proof.

The filibuster used to mean a senator talking for hours. Now it's a silent threat: say you object, and a bill needs 60 votes instead of 51. The Senate filed 336 of these motions in a single Congress.

Lobbying hit $4.5 billion in 2024.

A fix: bring back the talking filibuster, so blocking a bill takes real effort. And require a public cost-benefit check before any earmark gets attached to a bill.

Why it helps: it makes them argue in the open instead of blocking in silence.

Should senators have to stand up and talk?

## PLANT 10 — Biosphere
### The system punishes people for working more.

Some people on benefits turn down raises or extra hours, because earning a little more makes them lose more in benefits than they gain. 21.8% of recipients said they'd done it.

Meanwhile, big companies rarely face charges: in 2024, just 1% of white-collar prosecutions targeted a corporation.

A fix being piloted in 12 states: phase benefits out slowly, so every extra dollar you earn leaves you better off.

Why it helps: work should always pay.

Should benefits fade out instead of falling off a cliff?
